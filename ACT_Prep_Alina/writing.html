The Writing Essay

How ACT Writing works

You get 40 minutes and one prompt. The prompt names a contemporary issue and gives you three perspectives on it. Your job: develop your own perspective and analyze how it relates to at least one of the three you were given. Two readers each score your essay in four domains from 1 to 6; their domain scores are combined and reported on a 2–12 scale. It is optional and it does not affect your composite — it is reported on its own.

Say this to yourself before you start

This is a first draft written under time pressure, and the graders know it. They are not hunting for a polished essay. They are checking whether you can take a real position, reason about it, and wrestle honestly with a competing view. You do not need perfection. You need a clear argument that engages the other side.

The four domains — and what an 8 is missing

Every essay is judged on four things. Here is what each one looks like at a 4, a 5, and a 6 — in concrete behavior, not vague adjectives — so you know precisely what to aim your effort at.

DomainWhat it measures
Ideas & AnalysisDo you take a clear position and analyze the relationship between viewpoints, including tension and counterarguments?
Development & SupportDo you back claims with specific reasoning and concrete examples, not just restated opinion?
OrganizationIs there a clear arc, with each paragraph doing a distinct job and logical transitions between them?
Language UseAre your sentences varied and precise, with control of grammar, punctuation, and word choice?

Ideas & Analysis

4: States a clear thesis and shows some awareness that other perspectives exist.
5: Analyzes the relationship between your view and a given one, naming where they conflict.
6: Engages the tension directly — grants the strongest opposing point, then shows its limit, and frames the whole issue with insight.

Development & Support

4: Offers reasons and a general example or two.
5: Develops examples with some specificity and explains how they support the claim.
6: Uses precise, concrete evidence and follows each example through to why it proves the point — the reasoning is as strong as the example.

Organization

4: Has a beginning, middle, and end; paragraphs are mostly on topic.
5: Clear structure with functional transitions; ideas build in a sensible order.
6: The arc feels intentional — each paragraph earns its place, and transitions show the logical relationship between ideas, not just “next.”

Language Use

4: Clear enough to follow; some errors, some repetition.
5: Varied sentences and mostly precise word choice; errors are minor.
6: Controlled, varied, and precise; word choice does real work, and errors are rare enough to be invisible.

Why you are getting an 8, and how to get a 10

An 8 essay almost always does the same three things. It states a position clearly. It gives examples. And it mentions the other perspectives. What it does not do — and this is the entire difference — is genuinely engage the tension between the perspectives.

Concretely, here is what your 8-level essays are missing, and exactly how to fix each one:

  • You summarize the other view instead of arguing with it. An 8 says “Perspective One believes automation destroys jobs.” A 10 says “The strongest version of Perspective One is not that jobs vanish, but that the new jobs demand skills displaced workers do not have — and that is a real problem my position has to answer.” Grant the strongest form of the opposing point, then show its limit.
  • You avoid the strongest counterargument. Picking the weakest opposing perspective to knock down is obvious to a grader. Deliberately take on the hardest objection to your thesis. Beating the toughest case is what signals a 6 in Ideas & Analysis.
  • Your support is generic. “Technology has changed many industries” supports nothing. “When supermarkets added self-checkout, cashier hours fell but the store hired more staff to handle online-order pickup” is a specific claim a reader can picture. Specific beats grand, every time.
The single highest-leverage change

In one body paragraph, take the perspective you most disagree with, state its strongest point in a way its supporters would approve of, and then explain why your view still holds. Do that one thing well and you move from 8 to 10, because it lifts Ideas & Analysis and Development at once.

A structure that survives 40 minutes

Under time pressure you do not want to invent a structure — you want a reliable one you can pour ideas into. Five paragraphs, with a strict clock so you never run out of time mid-thought.

PhaseTimeWhat you are doing
Plan0:00–5:00Pick your thesis, choose which perspective to argue against, jot two specific examples.
Write5:00–37:00Intro, three body paragraphs, conclusion — about 6 minutes each.
Proofread37:00–40:00Fix the errors that cost easy points; add one transition if a jump feels abrupt.

Intro — frame the tension, then commit

Open by naming the real tension in the issue, then state a precise thesis that takes a side. Not “there are pros and cons.” A real claim.

Frame: “The debate over ___ is really a disagreement about ___. While it is tempting to ___, the stronger position is that ___.”

Body 1 — take on the opposition

Engage the perspective you most disagree with. Grant its strongest point honestly, then explain its limit. This is your money paragraph.

Frame: “Supporters of ___ are right that ___. But this overlooks ___, which is why ___.”

Body 2 — your strongest argument

Your best reason for your thesis, developed with one specific example carried all the way to why it proves your point.

Frame: “The clearest reason to hold this view is ___. Consider ___.”

Body 3 — add nuance

A complication or qualification that shows you see the issue in more than one dimension. Nuance is what a 6 in Ideas rewards.

Frame: “This does not mean ___. The harder question is ___, and here my view requires ___.”

Conclusion — raise the stakes, do not repeat

Restate what is at stake and what should follow from your position — do not just re-say your intro in new words. One or two sentences of “so what” is enough.

Frame: “If we get ___ wrong, the cost is ___. Getting it right means ___.”

Keep the frames flexible

These sentence frames are scaffolding, not a script. If every essay you write sounds identical, graders notice. Use the frame to get started, then let your own words take over. The architecture should be reliable; the sentences should sound like you.

What counts as good support

Development & Support is where an 8 leaks the most points, and it is fixable in one habit: replace vague grand claims with precise specific ones. You do not need real statistics — you need concrete examples a reader can picture.

History

Past shifts that echo the issue — the printing press, the assembly line, rural electrification.

Current events

Recent, well-known developments you can describe accurately without inventing numbers.

Science & tech

How a technology actually works and what it changes for the people who use it.

Literature

A book’s situation used as an analogy — sparingly, and only if it truly fits.

Personal observation

Something specific you have seen in your school, town, or job. Small and real beats big and vague.

Local detail

The self-checkout at your grocery store, the traffic app everyone in your town uses. Concrete and relatable.

Do not invent statistics

“Studies show 73% of workers…” is a bad idea. A made-up number is either obviously fake or simply unverifiable, and it adds nothing a reader trusts. A precisely described example is far more persuasive than a fabricated figure.

Vague claim, rewritten as specific support

Technology has changed the workplace in many ways.

When my mother’s clinic switched to digital records, she spent less time filing and more time with patients — the tool did not replace her, it changed what her day was made of.

History shows that people always fear new inventions.

When automatic telephone switching arrived, the operators it replaced were feared to be unemployable — yet the same networks created whole new categories of work within a generation.

Standardized tests do not measure everything about a student.

A test can score my algebra but not the three weeks I spent tutoring a classmate through it — the effort that actually made me learn.

Cameras in public can make people feel safer or watched.

The camera on our train platform likely deters the vandalism that used to close the station, but it also records every commuter who never did anything wrong.

Language Use — the easiest points

Of the four domains, Language Use is the one you can raise fastest, because it rewards habits rather than insight. You already write clearly. Tighten these and you pick up a domain point almost for free.

Vary your sentences

If three sentences in a row start with the subject and run the same length, combine two and start one with a dependent clause. Rhythm reads as control.

Use precise verbs

“The policy hurts workers” is weak. Displaces, strands, undercuts — a precise verb does the work of a whole phrase.

Transitions that show logic

“Also” and “another” just stack ideas. However, therefore, in contrast, as a result show the relationship — which is what Organization is graded on.

Cut the throat-clearing

Delete “In today’s society,” “Since the beginning of time,” and “I think that.” Start with the actual idea. Padding wastes minutes and reads as filler.

Your 3-minute proofreading checklist

  • Every sentence ends with punctuation and is a complete sentence (no fragments, no run-ons).
  • Subjects and verbs agree; verb tense is consistent.
  • Apostrophes: its (belonging) vs. it’s (it is); their/there/they’re.
  • Each paragraph has one clear job and a transition into the next.
  • Your thesis actually appears in the intro and is echoed in the conclusion.
  • Cross out the weakest sentence — it is usually padding.

Four practice prompts

These follow the real structure exactly: an issue paragraph, three perspectives, then the standard task. They are original — write full essays on them, timed, and use the samples below to grade yourself.

Prompt 1 — Automation and Work

Machines and software now perform tasks that people used to do — scanning groceries, sorting packages, answering routine questions, even drafting text. Supporters say automation frees people from dull, repetitive labor and lowers the cost of goods and services. Critics warn that it eliminates jobs faster than workers can retrain, and that the benefits flow to a few while the costs fall on many. As automation spreads into more kinds of work, societies must decide how to respond.

PerspectiveClaim
OneAutomation is progress. Every past wave of technology destroyed some jobs but created more, and standards of living rose. We should welcome it and not stand in its way.
TwoAutomation’s harm is unequal. The people displaced are rarely the people who gain, and telling them to “retrain” ignores how hard that is. We should slow it and protect workers.
ThreeThe technology is neutral; what matters is how we manage it. With the right training, safety nets, and rules, automation can lift almost everyone.

Task: Write a unified essay in which you develop your own perspective on automation and work. Analyze the relationship between your perspective and at least one other, and support your position with reasoning and specific examples.

Prompt 2 — The Role of Standardized Measurement

Schools, employers, and governments increasingly rank people and institutions by standardized measures — test scores, performance metrics, ratings. Supporters argue these numbers are fair because they apply the same yardstick to everyone. Critics counter that a single number flattens complex human qualities and pushes people to chase the metric instead of the thing it was meant to measure. As measurement spreads into more of life, we have to ask what it captures and what it erases.

PerspectiveClaim
OneStandardized measures are the fairest tool we have. They replace favoritism and gut feeling with objective, comparable data.
TwoWhat matters most cannot be counted. Reducing people to a number distorts behavior and rewards those who game the measure.
ThreeNumbers are useful but incomplete. They should inform decisions, never make them alone.

Task: Write a unified essay in which you develop your own perspective on standardized measurement. Analyze the relationship between your perspective and at least one other, and support your position with reasoning and specific examples.

Prompt 3 — Technology in Public Space

Cameras, sensors, and connected devices increasingly fill the places we share — streets, transit stations, parks, and stores. Supporters say this technology makes public spaces safer and more efficient, deterring crime and speeding up services. Critics argue it turns ordinary life into something constantly recorded, eroding the freedom to move through the world unwatched. Communities now face a choice about how much of their shared space should be monitored.

PerspectiveClaim
OneMonitoring makes everyone safer. If you are doing nothing wrong, the cameras protect you, and the data helps cities run better.
TwoConstant monitoring changes how people behave and who feels welcome. Privacy in public is a freedom worth protecting even at some cost to efficiency.
ThreeThe tools are here to stay; the real question is who controls the data and what limits we place on its use.

Task: Write a unified essay in which you develop your own perspective on technology in public space. Analyze the relationship between your perspective and at least one other, and support your position with reasoning and specific examples.

Prompt 4 — Individual Choice and Collective Benefit

Many of society’s hardest problems — conserving water in a drought, reducing waste, keeping a community healthy — depend on countless individual choices. Some argue that people should be free to decide for themselves and that persuasion, not pressure, is the right approach. Others argue that when individual choices add up to collective harm, communities are justified in requiring certain behavior. Where a society draws that line shapes everyday life.

PerspectiveClaim
OneIndividual freedom comes first. People should be informed and persuaded, but the choice must remain theirs.
TwoWhen personal choices cause shared harm, the community has the right to set rules. Freedom cannot mean freedom to damage everyone else.
ThreeThe best solutions make the good choice the easy choice — shaping options rather than forbidding them.

Task: Write a unified essay in which you develop your own perspective on individual choice and collective benefit. Analyze the relationship between your perspective and at least one other, and support your position with reasoning and specific examples.

Graded sample essays: 6, 8, 10

All three respond to Prompt 1 (Automation and Work). Read them back to back. The jump from the 8 to the 10 is the whole game — watch exactly what changes.

Sample at a 6 — states a position, generic support

Automation is a big topic in today’s society. Machines are doing more and more jobs that people used to do. I think automation is a good thing overall because it helps society move forward and makes life easier for everyone.

First, automation is good because it does the boring jobs that people do not want to do. For example, scanning groceries or sorting packages is very repetitive. If a machine does those things, then people do not have to. This is better for workers because they can do more interesting work instead. Technology has always helped people in many ways, and this is just one more example of that.

Second, automation makes things cheaper. When companies use machines, they do not have to pay as many workers, so they can sell things for less money. This is good for regular people because they can buy more with the money they have. A lot of stores use automation now and their prices are lower, which shows that automation helps customers.

Some people say that automation is bad because it takes away jobs. This is true that some people lose their jobs when machines come in. That is a bad thing for those people. But I still think automation is good because in the end there will be new jobs for people to do. History shows that people always fear new inventions but then things turn out fine.

In conclusion, automation is a good thing for society. It does the boring jobs, it makes things cheaper, and even though some people lose jobs, new jobs will be created. Society should welcome automation and not be afraid of it because it helps everyone in the long run.

Why this scored a 6 (roughly 3/3/4/4)

Ideas & Analysis (3): There is a clear thesis, but the opposing view is only mentioned and waved away (“new jobs will be created”) without real analysis of the tension. It never engages Perspective Two’s actual point — that the displaced are not the ones who gain.
Development (3): Examples are generic (“scanning groceries,” “a lot of stores”) and reasoning is thin. Claims like “technology has always helped” assert rather than support.
Organization (4): The five-paragraph shape is clear and orderly.
Language (4): Readable but repetitive; leans on “good,” “things,” and “a lot of.”
To reach an 8: Replace generic examples with one specific, developed case, and actually respond to the strongest version of the opposing view instead of dismissing it in a sentence.

Sample at an 8 — clear and organized, but does not engage the tension

Automation is reshaping the modern workplace, and people disagree sharply about whether that is a good thing. Supporters see progress; critics see displaced workers. My view is that automation is, on balance, a positive force, because throughout history new technology has expanded the total amount of work available even as it eliminated specific jobs.

The clearest reason to support automation is that it removes dangerous and repetitive labor. Consider manufacturing: tasks that once required workers to perform the same motion thousands of times a day, risking injury, are now done by machines. The workers who remain shift into roles that require judgment, such as monitoring and maintaining the equipment. This is not just cheaper for companies; it is genuinely better for the people who used to do the most grinding parts of the job.

Automation also lowers costs, which benefits ordinary consumers. When a grocery store installs self-checkout lanes, it can serve more customers with the same floor space, and the savings can be passed along in lower prices. People with limited budgets gain the most from cheaper goods, because a larger share of their income goes toward basic necessities.

Critics argue that automation destroys jobs and hurts workers, and it is true that some people lose their positions when machines arrive. This is a real cost and should not be ignored. However, I believe that with education and retraining, most workers can move into the new roles that technology creates. The economy has absorbed enormous technological change before, from the tractor to the computer, and unemployment did not rise permanently.

In conclusion, automation brings real benefits: safer work, lower prices, and new kinds of jobs. While the transition is painful for some, the long-term result is a more productive society. Rather than resisting automation, we should invest in helping workers adapt to it, so that its benefits can reach as many people as possible.

Why this scored an 8 (roughly 4/4/5/5)

Ideas & Analysis (4): Clear thesis, and it does acknowledge the opposing view — but it still summarizes and dismisses it (“with retraining, most workers can move”) rather than grappling with Perspective Two’s hardest claim: that the displaced worker and the person who gets the new job are usually different people. It never sits in that tension.
Development (4): The manufacturing and self-checkout examples are more specific than the 6, and the reasoning is followed a step further, but they stay at the level of “a store” rather than a vivid, particular case.
Organization (5): Well-structured with functional transitions.
Language (5): Varied sentences, mostly precise, few errors.
To reach a 10: In the counterargument paragraph, state the strongest version of Perspective Two — that retraining often fails the specific people displaced — grant it honestly, and only then show why your position survives it. Add one qualification that shows the answer is not automatic. That single move lifts Ideas and Development together.

Sample at a 10 — engages the tension, grants the counterargument, specific support

The argument over automation is usually framed as progress versus job loss, but that framing hides the real disagreement. Almost everyone agrees automation creates wealth and new work overall; the genuine dispute is about who receives the gains and who pays the costs. My position is that automation is worth embracing — but only if we are honest that its benefits and its harms fall on different people, and design our response around that fact. Perspective One’s optimism and Perspective Two’s alarm are both incomplete without it.

Perspective Two makes the strongest case against my view, and it deserves to be stated at full force. Its point is not simply that jobs disappear — it is that the worker displaced by a machine and the worker who fills the new job are rarely the same person. When a warehouse installs sorting robots, the fifty-year-old whose back is worn from lifting does not become a robotics technician; that job goes to someone younger with different training. Telling that worker to “retrain” can be, in practice, telling them their experience is worthless. This is a real harm, and any honest case for automation has to answer it rather than wish it away.

Yet the answer is not to slow the technology, because doing so freezes the harm in place without fixing it. The same warehouse that automates sorting can, if a community insists on it, fund the displaced worker’s transition, place them in the maintenance and logistics roles the robots create, and time the change so no one is dropped without a path forward. The productivity automation unlocks is precisely what makes such support affordable — you cannot cushion a transition you have refused to allow. This is where Perspective Three is right: the machine is not the villain or the hero. The outcome depends on the rules we build around it.

The clearest evidence is historical, but it cuts both ways, which is the point. When mechanized looms arrived, they did create a vast textile economy and cheaper cloth for everyone — Perspective One’s story is true. But the weavers who were replaced spent their own lifetimes worse off; the gains arrived for their children, not for them. Both things happened at once. The lesson is not “technology works out” or “technology destroys” — it is that the aggregate can improve while specific people are sacrificed, and whether we let that happen is a choice, not a law of nature.

So my view asks more than a simple yes or no. It requires holding two truths together: automation should move forward, because the wealth and safer work it produces are real, and it must be managed deliberately, because that wealth does not distribute itself. If we get this wrong, we repeat the weavers’ story and tell a generation of workers their loss is the price of everyone else’s progress. If we get it right, the people who bear the disruption are also among those who share in what it creates — and that, not the machines themselves, is what makes automation worth welcoming.

Why this scored a 10 (roughly 5/5/5/5, tipping to 6 in Ideas)

Ideas & Analysis (5–6): This is the difference. The essay reframes the debate (the real dispute is about distribution, not existence of gains), states the strongest opposing point in its most convincing form, grants it fully, and only then answers it. It weaves all three perspectives into a single argument instead of listing them. That is engaging the tension, not summarizing it.
Development (5): The warehouse worker and the mechanized-loom weavers are specific and carried all the way to why they prove the claim — including the crucial detail that the gains came for the next generation, not the displaced one. No invented statistics; the examples do the persuading.
Organization (5): Each paragraph has a distinct job, and transitions signal logical relationships (“Yet the answer is not,” “but it cuts both ways”).
Language (5): Controlled, varied sentences and precise verbs (“freezes,” “cushion,” “sacrificed”).
The unmistakable gap from the 8: The 8 said “critics have a point, but retraining solves it.” The 10 said “here is the critics’ hardest point, it is genuinely true, and my position has to — and does — answer it.” Same topic, same structure; the 10 argues with the opposition while the 8 only argues past it.

Your routine

You do not need to write ten essays. You need to make the 8-to-10 move automatic, and that takes a handful of timed reps with honest self-grading against the samples above.

The one-line takeaway

An 8 states a view and gives examples. A 10 takes the other side’s best point, admits it is real, and answers it anyway. Do that in one paragraph every time, and the score follows.
