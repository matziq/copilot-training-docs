The Science Section

Here is the thing almost nobody tells you: ACT Science is a reading-and-reasoning test wearing a lab coat. You already scored a 26 on it. Once you stop trying to remember science and start reading the data, that number moves fast. Let’s decide whether you take it at all, and then make it easy.

Should you even take the Science section?

The rules changed, so this is a real decision now — not an automatic yes. On the Enhanced ACT, your composite is the average of English, Math, and Reading only. Science is optional. It is reported on its own, and it feeds a separate STEM score (roughly, your Math and Science together). Whatever you get on Science, it cannot pull your composite down anymore.

The one fact that reframes everything

Under the old ACT, a bad Science section could sink your whole composite. That risk is gone. The only real cost of taking Science now is 40 minutes of fatigue right before the optional essay. The upside is optionality — a score you can send if a school wants it.

✓

Reasons to take it

  • Many colleges and scholarship programs still ask for a Science score or use the STEM score.
  • Direct-admit engineering, nursing, and health-science programs often want it specifically.
  • If you are applying to anything STEM, leaving Science blank can read as avoidance.
  • Your 26 is already respectable, and it is one of the easiest sections to raise, because it is a skill (reading graphs) rather than a body of memorized content.
✗

Reasons to skip it

  • It adds 40 minutes of hard concentration immediately before the Writing essay, when you are already tired.
  • If not one school on your list wants it, that time and energy is better spent on English, Math, and Reading — the only three sections that actually move your composite.
  • Prepping for it steals hours you could spend on Math, which is your lowest of the three that count.
Alina, here is my recommendation

Before you decide, open the admissions/testing page for every school on your list and write down what each one says about Science. Then default to taking it unless every single one explicitly says they do not want it. The downside is now only fatigue; the upside is a score you can choose to send. When the downside is small and the upside is real optionality, you take the option.

One more thing about time. If you do take Science, it should get a clearly smaller slice of your study hours than Math. Math is a content section that counts toward your composite and is your weakest of the three. Science is a technique section that does not count toward your composite. Prioritize accordingly: learn the Science method well, drill it a little, and pour the rest into Math.

How ACT Science actually works

The central insight, and the one that changes your score: about 95% of the questions are answered purely from the data on the page. Two, maybe three questions per test ask for outside knowledge, and those are basic. Everything else is “find the number, read the trend, compare the two lines.” You are not being tested on chemistry. You are being tested on whether you can read carefully under time pressure.

📊

Data Representation

🔬

Research Summaries

~2 passages. One or more experiments described, then questions about design, variables, and what would happen if you changed something.

💬

Conflicting Viewpoints

1 passage. Two or more scientists disagree about one phenomenon. This one is genuinely a reading passage, and it eats time.

Format facts

40 questions, 40 minutes, 6 passages, 4 answer choices each. No penalty for wrong answers, so never leave a blank — guess on anything you skip. Science is the fourth scored section on test day, after English, Math, and Reading, and before the optional Writing essay.

Your pacing plan

Six passages in 40 minutes is about 6 minutes 40 seconds each. Round it to “under 7 minutes” and set clock checkpoints so you never discover you are behind with two passages left. Do the short data passages fast to bank time for Conflicting Viewpoints.

The method — figures first

Do not read the passage top to bottom like a textbook. That is the single most common way people lose time on Science. Instead:

1. Go to the figures first

Before reading any prose, spend about 20 seconds per figure. For every table and graph, answer four questions: What is the independent variable (usually the x-axis or the left column)? What is the dependent variable (what was measured)? What are the units? And which direction is “more”?

2. Read prose only when forced

Most questions send you straight to a figure. Read the introductory paragraph only enough to know what the experiment is about. Dive into the detailed prose only when a question actually requires it — and it will tell you which figure or paragraph to look at.

3. Work the questions with a finger on the figure

For a “According to Figure 2…” question, put your finger (or pencil tip) on the exact row, point, or line. Trace it. The answer is a number or a direction you can literally point at.

4. When prose genuinely matters

On Conflicting Viewpoints, and on a few Research Summary questions, the words carry the meaning. For those, slow down and read carefully — but you have banked time from the fast figure passages to afford it.

Why this works

Reading the prose first makes you spend three minutes learning about a study, then re-read the figures anyway to answer questions. Figures-first means you only ever read the words you actually need. On a 40-minute section, that saved reading is the difference between finishing calm and guessing on the last passage.

Data Representation

These are the friendliest passages on the section. A short setup, then tables and graphs, then questions that mostly ask you to read a value or describe a trend. Here is how to read every kind of figure the ACT throws at you.

Tables

Read the column headers and units first. Find the row that matches the question, then the column, and read across. Watch for columns whose units differ (g vs. mg, s vs. min).

Scatter plots

Each dot is one measurement. Ask: as x increases, does the cloud of dots go up (direct), down (inverse), or nowhere (no relationship)? Do not try to read exact values from a loose scatter — read the trend.

Multi-line graphs

Every line has a label or a key. Before answering, physically confirm which line is which. Many questions are wrong-answered simply by tracing the wrong line.

Bar charts

Compare heights. Watch the y-axis: if it starts at, say, 40 instead of 0, small bars can look dramatically different than they really are.

Double-Y-axis graphs

Two different measurements share one x-axis, each with its own y-axis (left and right). Match each line to its own axis — the left scale for one line, the right scale for the other. Mixing them up is a classic trap.

Semi-log scales

The killer. On a log axis, the gridlines are 1, 10, 100, 1000 — each step is ten times the last, not a fixed amount. A point halfway between 10 and 100 is not 55; it is around 30. Read log axes by which decade a point sits in, not by linear distance.

How to read a semi-log axis without panic

See gridlines labeled 1, 10, 100, 1000? That is a log scale. The rule: equal spacing means equal multiplication. Moving up one gridline multiplies by ten. So a value one small step above 100 is a few hundred, not 101. When a question only asks “did it increase or decrease,” the log part does not matter — up is still up. It only matters when you must read or compare actual magnitudes.

Worked example

A chemist measured how much of Compound X dissolves in 100 mL of water at different temperatures.

Figure 1 — Solubility vs. temperature
Question

Based on Table 1, as the temperature rose from 20°C to 40°C, the solubility of Compound X increased by approximately how many grams per 100 mL?

A. 11   B. 17   C. 31   D. 62

Reasoning. Do not read the graph for this — the table gives exact numbers. At 20°C the solubility is 31 g; at 40°C it is 62 g. The increase is 62 − 31 = 31 g. The answer is C. Notice the traps built into the wrong answers: 62 (D) is the final value, not the change; 17 (B) is the jump from 30°C to 40°C; 11 (A) is the jump from 10°C to 20°C. The question asked for a difference between two specific rows, so subtract those two rows and nothing else.

Research Summaries

Here a passage describes one or more experiments, then asks about how they were designed and what the results mean. The words matter a bit more than in Data Representation, but the questions still lean on the data tables.

The vocabulary that unlocks these

Independent variable

What the researchers deliberately changed. It is usually the thing listed across the top of a table or along the x-axis.

Dependent variable

What they measured in response. This is the result, the number that came out of the experiment.

Control

The baseline condition where the thing being tested is absent or set to zero (no drug, no fertilizer, room temperature). It exists so you have something to compare against — without it, you cannot tell whether the treatment did anything.

What changes between Exp 1 and Exp 2

Usually the researchers hold most things constant and alter one new factor in Experiment 2. Find that one difference. Questions love to ask why the second experiment was run, and the answer is almost always “to test the effect of that one changed factor.”

The “what if they had done X” question

Some questions ask what the result would most likely be under a new condition the experiment did not test. You answer by extending the established trend. If growth rose as concentration went from 1 to 2 to 4, then a value of 3 falls between the 2 and 4 results, and a value of 8 continues past the last one in the same direction (unless the data already showed it leveling off or reversing).

Worked example

Students tested how fertilizer concentration affects the height of bean seedlings after three weeks. In Experiment 1 all plants were grown under low light. In Experiment 2 the same setup was repeated under high light. Everything else — soil, water, pot size, temperature — was held constant.

Table 2 — Mean seedling height after 3 weeks (cm)
Fertilizer (g/L)Experiment 1 (low light)Experiment 2 (high light)
0811
11217
21522
41420
Question 1

Which fertilizer concentration served as the control in both experiments, and why was it included?

Reasoning. The control is the 0 g/L row — the plants that got no fertilizer. It is there so the students can tell whether fertilizer itself caused any change in height. Without a no-fertilizer baseline, a tall plant tells you nothing, because you would not know how tall it would have grown anyway. Compare every treated row back to the 0 g/L row: in Experiment 1, 1 g/L raised height from 8 to 12 cm, so fertilizer helped.

Question 2

In Experiment 1, if the students had tested 3 g/L of fertilizer, the seedling height would most likely have been closest to:

A. 10 cm   B. 12 cm   C. 15 cm   D. 20 cm

Reasoning. Look at the trend in Experiment 1: height rose to a peak of 15 cm at 2 g/L, then fell to 14 cm at 4 g/L. So 3 g/L sits between the 2 g/L result (15) and the 4 g/L result (14) — somewhere around 14–15 cm. The closest choice is C, 15 cm. Choice D (20) belongs to Experiment 2, a classic “right number, wrong table” trap. This is why you always confirm which experiment a question is asking about before you read a value.

Conflicting Viewpoints

This is the one passage that is genuinely a reading test. There is little or no data — instead, two (sometimes three) scientists or students give competing explanations for the same phenomenon, and you answer questions about what each one believes.

Read the introduction carefully

The intro defines the shared phenomenon — the one thing everybody is trying to explain. If you misread this, every viewpoint after it will blur together. Spend real time here.

Summarize each view in five words

After each viewpoint, jot a tiny summary in the margin: “Scientist 1: humans hunted them out.” Five words. That summary is what you will actually use to answer questions, so you do not have to re-read the whole thing.

Do not do this passage first

Conflicting Viewpoints takes longer than any other passage because it is dense text. If you open the section with it, you burn your calm and your clock early. Do the quick Data Representation passages first to bank time, then come to this one. And when it is the only reading-heavy passage, it is fine for it to take 8–9 minutes.

The three question types here

  • “What would Scientist 1 say about …?” — Return to that scientist’s claim and reason from it. The answer must be consistent with their view, not with your own opinion.
  • Points of agreement/disagreement — Look for the fact both accept (usually stated in the intro) versus the interpretation they split on.
  • New evidence — A new finding is introduced; you decide whether it supports or weakens each view. Ask: does this fact fit the mechanism that scientist proposed?

Worked example

On a remote island, a species of large flightless bird vanished roughly 700 years ago, around the time humans first arrived. Two scientists explain the extinction differently.

Scientist 1. The birds disappeared because humans hunted them. The birds were large, slow, and had no prior experience with predators, so they did not flee people. Middens (ancient trash heaps) near the oldest human settlements are full of the birds’ bones. The extinction happened within about a century of human arrival — far too fast to be explained by gradual climate change.

Scientist 2. The birds disappeared because the island’s climate dried out. Pollen preserved in lake mud shows the wet forests the birds fed in were shrinking before humans arrived. As the forest gave way to open grassland, the birds lost their food supply. Humans may have taken a few, but the population was already collapsing from habitat loss.

Question

Suppose researchers discovered that the bird’s bones in the middens all date to after the forests had already shrunk to a small fraction of their former size. This finding would most directly:

F. weaken Scientist 1’s view   G. weaken Scientist 2’s view   H. support Scientist 2’s view   J. have no bearing on either view

Reasoning. Your five-word summaries: Scientist 1 — humans hunted them out; Scientist 2 — climate dried their forest. The new fact says the forest had already shrunk before the birds were being hunted. That fits Scientist 2’s claim that habitat loss came first and drove the collapse, with hunting as a late add-on. So it supports Scientist 2 — H. It does not directly weaken Scientist 1 (people could still have finished off a shrinking population), so F is weaker than H. Always pick the choice the evidence most directly matches.

The little outside knowledge you actually need

Only a couple of questions per test want prior knowledge, and the ACT reuses the same handful of basics. Learn this short list and you have covered essentially all of it. Do not go memorize a biology textbook — that time belongs to Math.

Science traps

The section is designed so that fast, careless reading produces a wrong answer that feels right. Here are the six that catch the most people.

1. Answering from the prose over the figure

When a sentence and a graph seem to conflict, the figure wins for “according to” questions. Go to the data the question points at, not your memory of the paragraph.

2. A y-axis that does not start at zero

A tiny difference can look huge when the axis runs from 40 to 50 instead of 0 to 50. Always read the axis numbers before you judge how big a change is.

3. “Increased” vs. “increased then leveled off”

A curve that rises and then flattens is not the same as one that keeps rising. Read the whole shape before you call it “increasing.”

4. Tracing the wrong line

On a multi-line graph, confirm which line matches the label before you read a value. Two adjacent lines are a setup for exactly this error.

5. Units that change between tables

Table 1 in grams, Table 2 in milligrams; Figure 1 in seconds, Figure 2 in minutes. If two figures disagree wildly, check the units before you conclude anything.

6. A reversed or logarithmic x-axis

If the x-axis runs high-to-low, “right” means “less,” so a rising line actually means the value falls as the variable increases. On a log axis, equal steps mean ×10, not +10.

Your routine

If you decide to take Science, you do not need to grind it. You need to internalize the method and then keep it sharp with short, regular reps. Learn the four passage moves, do a passage or two a few times a week, and let the full-length tests handle the endurance.
