Math

This is the most important page on the site, and it is not close. On the Enhanced ACT your composite is the average of English, Math, and Reading only — Science is reported on the side. That means your weakest section is now one third of your score instead of one quarter. Move Math from 24 to 28–30 and your whole composite moves with it. That is roughly 7 more correct questions out of 45. This guide shows you exactly where to find them.

The math that matters most

Composite = (English + Math + Reading) ÷ 3. At 28 English and 26 Reading, every point of Math is worth a full third of a composite point. Nothing else on this site pays back faster than the hour you spend here.

1. How ACT Math is actually built

Before you drill a single problem, know the shape of what you are walking into. Here is the format — the new Enhanced format, not the old one your old prep books describe.

45

Questions

Down from 60. Fewer questions, so each one is worth more.

50

Minutes

About 67 seconds per question on average — but you will not spend them evenly.

4

Answer choices

Not five. A blind guess is now worth 25%, not 20%.

0

Guess penalty

None, ever. Every bubble gets filled. A blank is a wasted 25%.

The calculator is permitted on the entire section. There is no no-calculator portion. And here is the fact almost no one tells students: the ACT does not give you a formula sheet. Every formula in Section 5 below, you carry in your head. That is why memorizing the formula bank is not optional — it is the price of admission.

What the 45 questions are made of

ACT sorts its math into two big buckets. You do not need to memorize the labels, but you should understand the balance, because it tells you what to drill.

Reporting categoryShareWhat it actually is
Integrating Essential Skills~40%The pre-algebra and geometry you learned years ago — rates, proportions, percents, area, averages — but wrapped in two or three steps so it looks harder than it is.
Preparing for Higher Math~60%Split across Number & Quantity, Algebra, Functions, Geometry, and Statistics & Probability. This is the content the rest of this page drills.

The single most useful structural fact: the questions run roughly easy to hard. Questions 1–15 are the friendliest problems on the test. Questions 35–45 are the hardest. This has two consequences that should reshape how you take the section:

Early questions: precision, not speed

A careless error on question 6 costs exactly as many points as one on question 44, but the question 6 error is preventable. These are points you already know how to get. Slow down enough to not throw them away.

Late questions: it is fine to guess

If questions 40–45 are genuinely beyond you right now, that is okay. Guess a letter, bubble it, and spend that time double-checking questions 1–30, where the points actually live for you.

2. The pacing plan

Sixty-seven seconds per question is the average, and averages lie. The smart plan spends time unevenly, because the early questions should take you far less than 67 seconds and the late ones need more. Here is the target distribution.

QuestionsTarget paceYou should reach it by…
1–15 (easy)~40 sec eachminute 12 → at question 15
16–30 (medium)~70 sec eachminute 30 → at question 30
31–45 (hard)whatever is leftminute 47 → leave 3 min to bubble & check

Write those three checkpoints — Q15 by 12, Q30 by 30, done by 47 — on your scratch paper the second the section starts. If the proctor puts the start time on the board, do the addition so you have real clock times, not elapsed minutes.

The two-pass method

Do not treat the section as one straight march from 1 to 45. Take two passes.

Pass 1 — harvest

Go in order. Answer everything you can solve in under about 90 seconds. The moment a problem looks like it will take longer — heavy setup, a diagram you have to build, four steps of algebra — guess a letter right now, bubble it, circle the number in your booklet, and move on. Never leave the bubble empty even for a second; if you run out of time the guess is already there working for you.

Pass 2 — hunt

With your first pass done and every bubble filled, go back to the circled questions. Now you have no time pressure hanging over easy points, and you can spend real minutes on the hard ones. If you crack one, erase the guess and bubble the real answer. If you do not, the guess stays.

Why "never blank" matters more now

With four choices, a blind guess is worth 25%. Across 45 questions, filling every remaining bubble instead of leaving five blank is worth, on average, more than one extra correct answer — for free, with zero knowledge. On the Enhanced ACT that stray point can be the difference between a 27 and a 28. There is no reason on earth to leave a blank.

Your one guess letter

Pick a single "letter of the day" for pure guesses — say C/H — and use it every time. Deciding fast beats deciding well on a blind guess, and one consistent letter means you never burn a second choosing.

3. Killing careless errors — your single biggest lever

Read this section twice. For a student at your level, the gap from 24 to 28 is not mostly a knowledge gap. A large share of the seven questions you need are ones you already know how to do and are simply getting wrong — because you solved for x when it asked for 2x, dropped a negative, used the diameter as the radius, or answered in the wrong units. These are the cheapest points on the entire test, because you do not have to learn anything. You have to stop leaking.

See it in action: the "wrong quantity" trap

If 5x − 3 = 12, then 5x = 15, so x = 3. But the question asks for x + 4. The answer is 7 — and you can bet 3 is sitting right there in the choices to catch you. You did the math perfectly and still got it wrong. Underlining "x + 4" before you start prevents this entirely.

Your last-three-minutes checklist

When you reach the end of pass 2 with a few minutes left, do not just sit there — run this, fast:

Bubbles

Scan the answer sheet top to bottom. Is every single one filled? Are any two answers crammed in one row? A shifted bubble column can cost ten questions — check it.

The circled ones

Re-read the actual question on two or three problems you were unsure of. Not the math — the question. Did it ask for x or 2x? Feet or inches? The true one or the false one?

Careless errors~65%
Genuine knowledge gaps~35%

A rough but honest split of where a 24-scorer’s missed questions come from. The good news is that the bigger slice is the one you can fix without learning any new math.

4. Using the calculator well

The calculator is a tool, not a strategy. Used well it wins you points; used lazily it slows you down and introduces typos. The rule of thumb: use it for ugly arithmetic and for graphing, not for things you should know by sight.

Genuinely faster on the calculator

  • Arithmetic on ugly numbers (17.5% of 384, √208)
  • Graphing to find x-intercepts and where two curves intersect
  • Checking whether a factored form is right
  • Testing answer choices (backsolving, below)

Slower on the calculator

  • 3 × 4, 12², half of 30 — know these cold
  • Simple fractions you can reduce by eye
  • Anything where typing the setup takes longer than the math
  • Recognizing 3-4-5 and 5-12-13 right triangles

Technique 1: Backsolving (plug in the answers)

When the question asks "what is x?" and the four choices are plain numbers, you do not have to solve forward. Plug the choices back in until one works. Because ACT lists numeric choices in ascending order, start with a middle one — the second or third — so a single test often tells you which direction to go.

Worked example — backsolving a quadratic

Question. Which value of x is a solution to x² − x = 12?   A) −4   B) −3   C) 3   D) 4

Do not factor. Test a middle choice, C = 3:   3² − 3 = 9 − 3 = 6. Too small — you need 12, so go bigger. Test D = 4:   4² − 4 = 16 − 4 = 12. ✓

The answer is D, found in two substitutions with no factoring and no chance of a sign error. (Factoring gives (x − 4)(x + 3) = 0, so x = 4 or x = −3 — both A and D would satisfy the full equation, which is exactly why the question asks for a single solution that is in the list.)

Technique 2: Picking numbers

When the answer choices contain variables instead of numbers, invent a number for the variable, compute the real answer, then see which choice matches. Avoid 0 and 1 (they make too many things look equal); a small number like 2 or 3 is ideal.

Worked example — picking numbers with variables in the choices

Question. The average of 5 numbers is n. A sixth number, 12, is added to the set. In terms of n, what is the new average?   A) (5n + 12)/6   B) (n + 12)/6   C) n + 2   D) (5n + 12)/5

Pick n = 6. Five numbers averaging 6 have a sum of 5 × 6 = 30. Add 12: new sum 42, new average 42 ÷ 6 = 7. Now plug n = 6 into each choice and find the one that gives 7:

  • A) (5·6 + 12)/6 = 42/6 = 7 ✓
  • B) (6 + 12)/6 = 3   ✗
  • C) 6 + 2 = 8   ✗
  • D) (5·6 + 12)/5 = 42/5 = 8.4   ✗

The answer is A. You never had to reason abstractly about "sum over count" — you turned an algebra problem into arithmetic.

One caution on picking numbers

If two choices both match your first number, that number was not discriminating enough. Pick a second, different number and test only the survivors. This is rare, but it happens — do not panic, just re-run.

5. The formula bank

Read this first

The ACT does not hand you a formula sheet. Every formula below you must know cold, from memory, on test day. Use the formula flashcards until these are reflexes, not recalls.

Lines & slope

slope m = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1)
slope-intercept: y = mx + b
point-slope: y − y1 = m(x − x1)
parallel: equal slopes  •  perpendicular: slopes multiply to −1

Distance & midpoint

d = √[(x2 − x1)2 + (y2 − y1)2]
midpoint = ( (x1 + x2)/2 , (y1 + y2)/2 )

The distance formula is just the Pythagorean theorem in disguise — the horizontal and vertical gaps are the legs.

Quadratics

Reading the discriminant

D > 0 → two real solutions. D = 0 → one (repeated) solution. D < 0 → no real solutions (two complex ones). ACT loves to ask "how many real solutions" — you never need the quadratic formula, just the sign of b² − 4ac.

Powers of i, fast

To find in, divide n by 4 and keep the remainder: remainder 1→i, 2→−1, 3→−i, 0→1. So i23: 23 ÷ 4 leaves remainder 3, so i23 = −i.

6. Functions in depth

Functions are the fastest-growing slice of ACT Math and the place where a 24-scorer most often loses points to unfamiliarity rather than carelessness. The good news: the notation is scarier than the ideas.

Notation and evaluation

f(x) is just a rule with a name. To evaluate f(3), replace every x with 3. Nothing more. If f(x) = x² − 2x + 1, then f(3) = 9 − 6 + 1 = 4. When the input is itself an expression, substitute the whole thing and keep parentheses: f(a + 1) = (a + 1)² − 2(a + 1) + 1.

Composition

f(g(x)) means "do g first, then feed the result into f." Work from the inside out and it is easy; work carelessly and you invert the order.

Worked example — composition, both directions

Let f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x².

f(g(3)): inside first, g(3) = 9. Then f(9) = 2(9) + 1 = 19.

g(f(3)): inside first, f(3) = 7. Then g(7) = 7² = 49.

The two answers differ — order matters. ACT will happily list both 19 and 49 as choices to see if you composed in the wrong order.

Domain and range

Domain = the x-values you are allowed to plug in. Range = the y-values that can come out. Two red flags shrink a domain: you cannot divide by zero and you cannot take an even root of a negative. So f(x) = 1/(x − 4) excludes x = 4, and f(x) = √(x − 2) requires x ≥ 2.

The four transformations

Starting from y = f(x), each tweak moves the graph in a predictable way. The one that trips up almost everyone is horizontal shifts, because they go the opposite direction from what the sign suggests.

ChangeEffect on the graph
f(x) + kshifts up k (down if k is negative)
f(x) − kshifts down k
f(x + h)shifts LEFT h — the counterintuitive one
f(x − h)shifts RIGHT h
−f(x)flips over the x-axis (upside down)

Figure 2 — Horizontal shift: f(x + 3) moves LEFT

Why left? f(x + 3) reaches its bottom when x + 3 = 0, i.e. at x = −3 — three units to the left of where the original bottomed out (x = 0). The input hits its "special" value sooner, so the whole picture slides left. Say it to yourself every time: "plus inside, move left."

Intercepts and the vertex

The x-intercepts are where y = 0 (set the function equal to zero and solve). The y-intercept is where x = 0 (plug in zero). For a parabola y = ax² + bx + c, the vertex sits at x = −b/(2a); plug that x back in to get the vertex’s y. On the calculator you can also just graph it and read the intercepts and the minimum/maximum directly.

Worked example — vertex of a parabola

Find the vertex of y = 2x² − 8x + 3.

x-coordinate: x = −b/(2a) = −(−8)/(2·2) = 8/4 = 2.

y-coordinate: y = 2(2)² − 8(2) + 3 = 8 − 16 + 3 = −5.

Vertex = (2, −5). Because a = 2 > 0, the parabola opens up, so this is a minimum.

Piecewise and inverse functions

A piecewise function uses different rules on different intervals; the only skill is choosing the right rule for the input you are given, then evaluating normally. An inverse function undoes the original: to find f−1(x), swap x and y, then solve for y. If f(x) = 3x − 6, then y = 3x − 6 → x = 3y − 6 → y = (x + 6)/3, so f−1(x) = (x + 6)/3.

How the ACT disguises functions

It rarely says "compose these functions." Instead it gives you a table of values and asks for f(g(2)) — you read g(2) off the table, then look up f of that. Or it draws a graph and asks "for how many values of x does f(x) = 3?" — you draw the horizontal line y = 3 and count crossings. Recognize the disguise and the problem collapses.

7. Geometry & trigonometry in depth

Geometry rewards drawing. If a problem describes a figure and does not give you one, sketch it. If it gives you one, mark it up with every length and angle you know. Most ACT geometry is a chain of small, known facts.

Angles with parallel lines

When a transversal cuts two parallel lines, it creates only two different angle sizes, and they are supplementary (add to 180°). Corresponding angles are equal; alternate interior angles are equal; same-side interior angles sum to 180°. In practice: mark every angle as either "the big one" or "the small one," and you can label the whole figure from a single given angle.

Figure 3 — Parallel lines cut by a transversal

Triangle rules you use constantly

  • The three angles sum to 180°.
  • An exterior angle equals the sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles.
  • Triangle inequality: any two sides must sum to more than the third. So sides 3 and 8 force the third side between 5 and 11.
  • The largest angle is opposite the longest side.

Similarity and congruence

Similar triangles have equal angles and proportional sides. Set up a proportion of matching sides and solve. Remember how scaling works: if a figure is enlarged so each side is k times longer, its area grows by k² and its volume by k³. Doubling the sides of a box multiplies its volume by eight, not two — a favorite ACT question.

Special right triangles in action

Worked example — a 30-60-90 in a word problem

A ramp rises at 30° and its base (the horizontal distance) is 12√3 feet. How high is the top of the ramp?

The ramp is the hypotenuse; the base is the side opposite the 60° angle (the "√3" side); the height is the side opposite the 30° angle (the "1" side). Using the ratio 1 : √3 : 2, the base corresponds to √3·(short side). So √3 · (short side) = 12√3, giving short side = 12. The height is 12 feet.

Circles, fully

The parts to know: a central angle has its vertex at the center; an inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle. An inscribed angle is half the central angle that cuts off the same arc. A chord connects two points on the circle; a tangent touches at exactly one point and is perpendicular to the radius drawn to that point.

Figure 4 — Central angle vs. inscribed angle on the same arc
Worked example — arc length and sector area

A circle has radius 6. A sector has a central angle of 60°. Find its arc length and its area.

Arc length = (60/360) · 2π(6) = (1/6) · 12π = 2π ≈ 6.28.

Sector area = (60/360) · π(6)² = (1/6) · 36π = 6π ≈ 18.85.

The whole game is the fraction θ/360 — the sector is that fraction of the full circle.

Coordinate geometry

Most coordinate problems reduce to three tools: slope, the distance formula, and the midpoint formula (all in Section 5). To show a triangle is right, check that two sides have slopes that multiply to −1. To find a circle’s center and radius from (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r², read off (h, k) and √(r²) — watch the signs, since (x + 2)² means h = −2.

Right-triangle trig — when to reach for SOH CAH TOA

Use it whenever you have a right triangle and you are mixing an angle with sides. Pick the ratio that connects what you know to what you want. Know one side and an angle, want another side? That is sine, cosine, or tangent.

Worked example — law of cosines

A triangle has two sides of length 5 and 7 with a 60° angle between them. Find the third side.

c² = 5² + 7² − 2(5)(7)cos 60° = 25 + 49 − 70(0.5) = 74 − 35 = 39.

c = √39 ≈ 6.24. Use the law of cosines whenever you know two sides and the angle between them (or all three sides and want an angle).

"Figure not drawn to scale"

When you see those words, trust only the numbers and stated relationships, never how the picture looks. An angle drawn to look like 90° may be 80°; a side that looks longer may be shorter. The figure is a rough map, not a measurement. Redraw it yourself if the given numbers contradict what you see.

8. The traps ACT Math sets on purpose

ACT is not trying to be fair-minded about wrong answers. Every incorrect choice is engineered — it is the number you would land on if you made a specific, predictable mistake. Learn the traps and you can often spot the wrong answers before you finish solving.

1. The intermediate value

You compute x = 3 on the way to the real answer, 2x = 6. The choice "3" is there waiting. Fix: underline what is asked; solve to the end.

2. Radius vs. diameter

Given a diameter of 10, the choice built on r = 10 (instead of r = 5) is planted. Area with r = 10 gives 100π; the real answer is 25π. Fix: write r = ___ before any circle formula.

3. Not drawn to scale

The figure is deliberately misleading so eyeballing fails. Fix: use only stated numbers; ignore appearances.

4. Average speed

Average speed is total distance ÷ total time, never the mean of the two speeds. Fix: compute distance and time separately, then divide.

5. Percent up then down

Up 20% then down 20% does not return you to start. 100 → 120 → 96. A net 4% loss. Fix: apply percents in sequence to actual numbers.

6. Expression vs. variable

It asks for the value of (x + y), not x. Solving for x alone lands on a trap. Fix: circle the exact expression requested.

7. Fencepost / inclusive counting

Integers from 20 to 40 inclusive: it is 40 − 20 + 1 = 21, not 20. Fix: add 1 when both ends are included.

8. Units that change mid-question

Speed in feet per second, time in minutes, answer in miles. Fix: convert everything to one unit first, and circle the units in the choices.

Worked example — the average speed trap

You drive 60 miles at 30 mph, then 60 miles at 60 mph. What is your average speed for the whole trip?

The tempting (wrong) answer is (30 + 60)/2 = 45 mph. Do it properly: the first leg takes 60/30 = 2 hours, the second takes 60/60 = 1 hour. Total distance 120 miles, total time 3 hours. Average speed = 120/3 = 40 mph. You spend more time at the slow speed, so the true average leans toward the slow end — never the simple mean.

Worked example — percent increase then decrease

A $200 jacket is marked up 25%, then that new price is discounted 25%. What is the final price?

Markup: 200 × 1.25 = 250. Discount: 250 × 0.75 = $187.50 — not $200. A 25% gain followed by a 25% cut always ends below the start, because the cut applies to a bigger number than the gain did.

Worked example — "at least one" via the complement

You flip a fair coin 3 times. What is the probability of getting at least one head?

Computing "exactly 1, exactly 2, exactly 3 heads" is slow and error-prone. Instead use the complement: P(at least one head) = 1 − P(no heads) = 1 − P(all tails). P(all tails) = (1/2)³ = 1/8. So the answer is 1 − 1/8 = 7/8. Whenever you see "at least one," reach for 1 − P(none) first.

Worked example — weighted average

In a class, tests count for 70% of the grade and homework for 30%. A student averages 80 on tests and 90 on homework. What is the course grade?

Grade = 0.70(80) + 0.30(90) = 56 + 27 = 83. It leans toward tests because they carry more weight — the plain average of 85 is the trap answer.

Worked example — combinations vs. permutations

A club of 5 members must choose a 2-person committee. How many committees are possible?

Order does not matter (a committee of A and B is the same as B and A), so use combinations: 5C2 = 5! / (2!·3!) = (5·4)/(2·1) = 10. If instead they were choosing a president and a vice-president, order would matter and you would use 5P2 = 20.

Worked example — exponential growth

A town of 500 people grows 8% per year. What is the population after 3 years, to the nearest person?

Population = 500(1.08)³ = 500(1.259712) ≈ 629.86, so about 630 people. Growth is multiplicative, not additive — do not just add 8% of 500 three times, because each year’s growth is figured on the larger new total.

9. Your 26-day Math routine

Reading this guide once will not move your score. Repetition on the exact topics you miss will. Here is the loop that actually works, and it is short: drill a topic, get some wrong, read the explanation, log the rule, and re-drill the same topic three days later. The three-day gap is what forces your brain to actually store it instead of recognizing it in the moment and forgetting it by dinner.

1

Topic drills →

Short, targeted sets on one skill at a time. This is where most of your seven points come from.
2

Practice tests →

Three full 45-question sections to build pacing and stamina under a real clock.
3

Formula flashcards →

Because there is no formula sheet, these must become reflexes. Run them daily.
The daily loop, concretely

Pick a topic. Do a drill. For every miss, write the rule you broke — not "I did it wrong" but "I used diameter as radius." Re-drill that same topic in three days. When you can clear a set with no careless misses twice, retire it and move to the next. Follow the ordering in the day-by-day plan and you will hit each weak topic at least twice before September 19.
