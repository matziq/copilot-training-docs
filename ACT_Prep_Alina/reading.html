Reading — Getting from 26 to 29

The single most important fact about your Reading section is that the Enhanced ACT gives you more time than the old test did — about 20% more per question. That changes everything. The old advice was "skim and hunt" because you were starving for time. You are not starving anymore. You can actually read the passage, and for a 26 who loses points to misreading rather than to running out of clock, that is exactly the fix.

The math on your goal

A 26 is roughly 27–28 of 36 correct. A 29 is roughly 30–31 of 36. That is two or three more questions across the whole section. You are not far. The gains come from one place above all others: recognizing why a wrong answer is wrong instead of choosing the one that "sounds right." That is what the wrong-answer section trains.

How ACT Reading works now

Four passages, nine questions each, 40 minutes. The passages always come in the same order, and knowing the order lets you plan:

The four passages, always in this order
#TypeWhat it isWhat it tests hardest
1Literary NarrativeProse fiction — a scene from a story or novelImplied emotion, relationships, character
2Social ScienceEconomics, psychology, history, sociologyArgument and evidence
3HumanitiesArt, music, literature, personal essay, memoirTone and authorial voice
4Natural ScienceBiology, chemistry, physics, earth scienceMechanism and sequence

One of the four is always a paired set — two shorter passages, Passage A and Passage B, by different authors on a related topic, followed by questions about each one and then questions that compare them. It can land on any of the four types, though it most often shows up in Social Science or Natural Science.

Why the extra time matters so much for you

On the old ACT you had about 52 seconds per question and had to gamble. Now you have roughly 67 seconds — enough to read a passage for real (about 3 minutes) and still spend 6–7 minutes answering, returning to the text for each one. A 26 who reads carefully and checks the text will beat a 30-level reader who is still skimming out of old habit. The time is there. Use it to be sure, not fast.

The method, step by step

There are two schools of thought on Reading: "read the passage first" and "go straight to the questions and hunt." For you, on this format, read the passage first. Here is the exact approach and why it wins.

Step 1 Read for structure & tone (~3 min)

Read the whole passage at a steady pace. You are not memorizing details — you will come back for those. You are building a map: what is the main point, how does the author feel about it, and where does the passage turn? Detail lookup is easy when you know where things live.

Step 2 Answer from the text (~6–7 min)

Do every question with your finger on the line. For detail and inference questions, go back and read the relevant sentences before you look at the choices. If you cannot point to the words that prove your answer, it is not your answer.

Why "skip the passage and hunt" backfires

The hunt method can work on Natural Science, where answers sit in specific findable lines. It falls apart on Literary Narrative and Humanities, where the questions ask what a character means, how the author feels, or what the passage implies — things that live in the whole passage, not in one line you can jump to. If you skip the reading on those two, you answer by vibe and the distractors eat you alive. Since the extra time removes the reason to skip, don’t.

Track three things as you read

The main claim — if you had to say the passage’s point in one sentence, what is it? The author’s attitude — approving, critical, neutral, amused, uneasy? The shifts — the moment the passage changes direction, usually marked by but, however, yet, or a new paragraph. Those three notes answer a surprising share of the questions by themselves.

The 10-minute checkpoint

Budget 10 minutes per passage and check the clock as you finish each one. Finished passage 1 by the 10-minute mark, passage 2 by 20, passage 3 by 30. If a passage is going badly — you have read it twice and it still will not resolve — do not sink five minutes into one question. Bubble your best guess, flag it, and move on. A passage where you nail 7 of 9 quickly is worth more than one where you claw for a ninth point and blow your timing on the rest.

The five ways a wrong answer is wrong

This is the most valuable section on the page. On Reading, three of the four choices are wrong on purpose, and they are wrong in predictable ways. Strong readers still miss questions because they choose the answer that sounds best. You will do better by eliminating — hunting for the flaw in each wrong choice — than by reaching for the one that feels right. Here are the five flaws to hunt for.

1. Too extreme

The choice overstates. Watch for absolute words: always, never, all, none, proves, entirely, impossible. Passages hedge; extreme answers do not. If the text says a policy "helped in some cities," the answer that says it "solved urban poverty completely" is too extreme, even though it is about the right topic.

2. Right idea, wrong paragraph

The statement is true — the passage really does say it — but it does not answer this question, which is about a different part of the text. The ACT rewards you for reading the true fact and forgetting to check that it is relevant here. Always confirm the choice answers the question that was asked, in the location it asked about.

3. Distortion

The most dangerous trap. The choice reuses real words and names from the passage but reverses or twists the relationship between them. The passage says the drought caused the migration; the choice says the migration caused the drought. All the vocabulary is familiar, so it feels safe. Check the direction of every cause, comparison, and claim.

4. Half-right

Two clauses, one true and one false. The first half matches the passage perfectly, which makes you stop reading and pick it; the second half quietly contradicts the text. The narrator admires her mentor and resents his fame. If she never resents his fame, the whole choice is wrong — a half-right answer is a wrong answer. Read every word of every choice.

5. Out of scope

The choice is reasonable in the real world but never stated in the passage. Your outside knowledge agrees with it, so it feels correct. But Reading is a closed-book test of this text: if the passage did not say it or clearly imply it, it is out of scope, no matter how true it is in life.

Worked example: spotting all five flaws on one question

PFor most of the nineteenth century, the little town of Harrow lived by its mill. When the river was rerouted in 1887, the mill’s wheel fell still, and within a decade half the families had gone west. Those who stayed did not mourn the mill so much as the certainty it had given them — the sense that tomorrow would look like today. The historian Clara Bowen, writing in 1961, argued that Harrow’s decline was less an economic event than a psychological one: a town losing not its livelihood but its faith that the future was knowable.

Question. According to the passage, Bowen argues that Harrow’s decline was primarily:

A. an economic collapse caused entirely by the loss of the mill.
B. a psychological loss of the town’s confidence in a predictable future.
C. proof that river towns always fail once their industry moves.
D. the result of families choosing to move west for better jobs.

Answer: B. The passage says Bowen called the decline "less an economic event than a psychological one" and defined it as losing "faith that the future was knowable." B matches exactly.

  • A is a distortion (and too extreme): it says "economic…entirely," but Bowen argued the decline was less economic than psychological. It reverses her point and adds "entirely."
  • C is too extreme and out of scope: "always fail" is absolute, and the passage never generalizes to all river towns — only Harrow.
  • D is right idea, wrong question: families really did move west (true from the passage), but that is not what Bowen argues the decline primarily was. It answers a different question.

Notice how you got to B without ever "liking" it — you convicted the other three.

The question types and how to attack each

Main idea

Asks for the central point of the passage or a paragraph. The answer covers the whole thing, not one vivid detail. A choice that is true but only describes one paragraph is too narrow; a choice that reaches beyond the passage is too broad. If you tracked the main claim while reading, this is nearly free.

Explicit detail

The answer is stated outright in the text. Go back and find it — do not answer from memory, because the distractors are built from your fuzzy recollection. These are the questions the extra time is for: read the lines, confirm the words, choose.

Inference ("most strongly suggests," "implies," "the reader can conclude")

An inference on the ACT is not speculation — it is a small, safe step that still requires textual proof. The correct inference is almost obvious once you find the right lines; if a choice requires a leap or a story you invented, it is wrong. Ask: which choice could I defend by pointing at the passage? That one.

Vocabulary in context

The method: go back to the line, cover the word, read the sentence, supply your own word that fits, then match your word to the choices. The trap is that the word’s most common meaning is usually the wrong answer — the ACT tests secondary meanings. If "the tax was a heavy charge" is tested, charge means burden, not an electric charge or a criminal accusation.

Function / purpose ("the author includes X in order to")

Asks why a detail is there, not what it says. Zoom out one level: how does this sentence serve the paragraph around it? A quoted statistic usually supports the claim just before it; an anecdote usually illustrates a general point. Read the sentences on both sides of the cited detail.

Tone and point of view

Asks how the author or narrator feels. Answers cluster in the Humanities passage, where voice is strong. Match the emotional temperature: a gently amused essay is not "furious," and a measured analysis is not "passionate." Build a working vocabulary of ACT tone words:

wry ambivalent skeptical reverent measured bemused nostalgic detached earnest critical admiring cautious — know what each one actually means, because the ACT counts on students guessing.

Comparative questions (paired passage)

Ask how the two passages relate: where they agree, where they disagree, how one author would react to the other. Do them last, after you understand each passage on its own. More on this below.

The paired passage

The paired set intimidates students because it looks like twice the work. It is not, if you attack it in the right order. Do not read both passages at once and try to hold two arguments in your head — you will blur them.

1 Passage A, then its questions

Read Passage A. Answer only the questions that are about Passage A alone (they usually come first). Lock in A’s main claim and attitude before you touch B.

2 Passage B, then its questions

Now read Passage B and answer the B-only questions. Note in one phrase how B’s view differs from A’s — that phrase is what the comparison questions will test.

3 The comparison questions last

Only now do the questions that mention both. You already understand each passage, so comparing them is quick.

Comparison questions ask a narrow set of things, and it helps to know them in advance:

  • Points of agreement: what would both authors accept as true?
  • Points of disagreement: where do they part ways — on facts, on interpretation, on what matters?
  • Cross-response: how would Author A likely react to a specific claim in Passage B? Anchor this in A’s stated view, not your guess about A’s personality.
  • Difference in method or emphasis: one author uses data while the other uses anecdote; one focuses on causes while the other focuses on consequences.

What each of the four passage types demands

Literary Narrative

Track relationships and implied emotion. The questions ask what a character means as opposed to what they literally say, and how characters feel about each other. Read for subtext: a line of dialogue often reveals the opposite of its surface. This passage rewards reading the whole scene, not hunting lines.

Social Science

Track the argument and its evidence, and keep the author’s own view separate from views they are merely reporting. The classic trap: a claim the author describes in order to criticize gets offered as the author’s own position. Ask, "Whose idea is this — the author’s, or someone the author is quoting?"

Humanities

Expect a strong authorial voice, so tone and attitude questions cluster here. This is where your tone vocabulary earns its keep. Read for how the author feels about the subject, and do not flatten a nuanced, mixed feeling (ambivalent, bittersweet) into a simple like/dislike.

Natural Science

Track mechanism and sequence — what causes what, in what order. And do not let your own science knowledge override the text: if the passage describes a process a certain way, answer from the passage even if you learned it differently in class. Cause-and-direction distortions are common here.

Worked example: a Literary Narrative "what she means" question

PWhen Priya laid the acceptance letter on the kitchen table, her father read it twice, folded it along its original creases, and set it back down exactly where she had put it. "Boston," he said, as if naming a distant weather system. He rose to refill the kettle that was already full. Priya watched his back and understood that he had been rehearsing pride for weeks and could not, now that the moment had come, find the shape of it.

Question. The narrator’s description of the father refilling "the kettle that was already full" primarily suggests that he is:

A. absentminded and prone to forgetting simple tasks.
B. searching for something to do because he is struggling to express his feelings.
C. angry that Priya has decided to move so far from home.
D. indifferent to the news in the letter.

Answer: B. The final sentence tells us directly that he "had been rehearsing pride" but "could not…find the shape of it." Refilling a full kettle is a nervous, occupying gesture — a man who feels a great deal and cannot say it, so his hands look for a job.

  • A (out of scope): nothing suggests he is generally forgetful; the passage gives a specific emotional cause, not a character flaw.
  • C (distortion / too extreme): "angry" contradicts "rehearsing pride." The text shows love that cannot find words, not anger.
  • D (contradicts the text): he read the letter twice and had rehearsed for weeks — the opposite of indifferent.

Timing and triage

Forty minutes, four passages, ten minutes each. Simple to say, easy to blow if one passage swallows your clock. Here is how to protect your score.

Your clock checkpoints
Time elapsedYou should be…
10:00finishing passage 1
20:00finishing passage 2
30:00finishing passage 3
40:00done, every bubble filled

Reorder if fiction slows you down

The passages are printed in a fixed order, but you can do them in any order you like. If Literary Narrative always eats your time, start with the passage type you read fastest — often Natural Science or Social Science — bank the easy points, and come back to fiction with a calm clock. Just bubble carefully so your answers land on the right questions when you jump around.

With five minutes left

Stop trying to finish a passage perfectly. Scan for the quickest remaining points — explicit-detail and vocabulary questions — and grab those first. Then make sure every bubble is filled. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so a blank is a wasted 25% chance. If you must guess, pick one letter and use it for all your guesses; it is faster and slightly more consistent.

Never leave a blank

This is the cheapest advice on the site and the most often ignored under pressure. Before time is called, run your eyes down the bubble sheet. Any empty circle gets a guess. On 36 questions, filling four blind guesses will, on average, hand you a free question — and one question is a chunk of the gap between 26 and 29.

Your routine

Reading improves through reps with real review, not through reading more novels this month. The gains come from studying why you missed, in the language of the five wrong-answer types.

Every study day

One timed passage (10 minutes). Then, for each miss, name the flaw: was your wrong choice too extreme, distorted, half-right, out of scope, or right-idea-wrong-place? Naming it is what stops you repeating it.

Twice a week

A full 4-passage timed section, so pacing and stamina stay honest. Practice reordering — do your fastest passage type first — until it feels automatic.

The last week

Reread the five wrong-answer types and the tone vocabulary. Do light timed passages to stay warm. Learn nothing new after Wednesday.

The one thing to remember

You have more time than the old ACT gave — enough to actually read the passage and prove every answer from the text. Stop choosing the answer that sounds right and start convicting the three that are wrong. Do that on two or three more questions per section and you are at 29.
