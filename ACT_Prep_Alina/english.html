English — Getting from 28 to 31

You already have the instincts of a strong English student. A 28 means you catch most errors on feel. The problem is that "on feel" tops out around here. To reach 30 or 31 you need to stop guessing on the handful of questions that split good scorers from great ones — and those are almost never the comma questions. They are the rhetoric questions. This guide covers everything, but it spends the most energy where your points actually are.

How ACT English works

The English section hands you five passages, each about the length of a short essay, with words and phrases underlined and numbered. Most questions show you the underlined part and offer four ways to write it: the first is always NO CHANGE, and the other three are alternatives. Your job is to pick the version that is correct, clear, and appropriate for the passage — or to keep NO CHANGE when nothing is wrong. A minority of questions instead ask a real question in a box: they point at a spot ("Which choice best introduces the paragraph?") or step back and ask about the whole essay.

Underneath, the test measures two things in roughly equal amounts:

Half Conventions of Standard English

Punctuation, usage, and sentence structure. Rule-based. There is a right answer and you can prove it. This is where your 28 is already living, and where a couple of blind spots are quietly costing you points.

Half Production of Writing & Knowledge of Language

Relevance, organization, transitions, concision, and word choice. Judgment-based, but not subjective — every question has one defensible answer. This is the half that separates 28 from 32, and most students never study it because it feels like "just reading."

Notice the implication: if you only drill grammar rules, you are studying the half you are already good at. The fastest points on this test, for you specifically, are in the second box.

The method

Read the whole sentence, not the underlined fragment

The underline is bait. The error — or the fix — almost always depends on something outside the underline: the subject three words earlier, the verb tense of the previous sentence, the noun a pronoun points back to. If your eyes only touch the underlined words, you will miss agreement and modifier errors every time. Read from the capital letter to the period.

Substitute each choice and hear the full sentence

Do not evaluate the four options against each other in the abstract. Plug each one into the sentence and read the whole thing. The wrong answers often look fine sitting alone in the answer column and only fall apart when you actually run them through the sentence.

The three principles that decide most questions

1. Two choices that mean the same thing are both wrong

The test has one answer. If and so and therefore would both work, neither can be right — you would have two correct answers. When two options are interchangeable, eliminate both and look at what the other two are doing differently.

2. The shortest correct option usually wins

The ACT rewards concision. When several choices are grammatically fine, the briefest one is almost always the answer. Length is a tiebreaker, not a rule — a short wrong answer is still wrong — but it settles a lot of questions.

3. Cut anything the sentence does not need

If a choice adds a detail that is already implied or already stated, that detail is redundant and the choice is wrong. Watch for the answer that simply deletes the underlined words — it is right far more often than students expect.

Pacing: 7 minutes a passage

Five passages, 35 minutes, so budget 7 minutes each and glance at the clock after passages 2 and 4. English is the one section where most students finish early, so do not rush — rushing is what turns your careful 28 into a careless 26. If you fall behind, speed up on the pure grammar questions (they are fast once you see the rule) and protect your time for the "Which choice…" questions, which actually reward thinking.

Punctuation, in full

Punctuation questions look intimidating because a single item can offer a comma, a semicolon, a colon, a dash, and NO CHANGE all at once. They are actually the most learnable questions on the test, because the rules are mechanical. Learn the rules below and you can prove your answer instead of guessing.

The comma splice — the error the ACT loves most

You cannot join two complete sentences (independent clauses) with only a comma. The rain stopped, we went outside. Fix it four ways: a period (The rain stopped. We went outside.), a semicolon (The rain stopped; we went outside.), a comma plus a FANBOYS conjunction (The rain stopped, so we went outside.), or by making one clause dependent (When the rain stopped, we went outside.).

The four legitimate jobs of a comma

A comma is only correct if it is doing one of these:

1. Separating items in a series: We packed apples, pears, and figs.
2. After an introductory word or phrase: After the storm, the streets flooded.
3. Setting off nonessential information: My brother, who lives in Ohio, called last night.
4. Before a FANBOYS conjunction that joins two independent clauses: I studied for weeks, and I passed easily.

If a comma is not doing one of those four jobs, it is probably wrong. That single test eliminates a huge share of comma answers.

When not to use a comma

Never between a subject and its verb. The students in the front row, answered first. The students in the front row answered first.

Never before a preposition just because you hear a pause. The ACT plants commas at natural breathing spots that are grammatically illegal. She walked quietly, into the room. She walked quietly into the room.

Never before the and in a two-item pair. A comma before and is only correct when a full sentence follows it. She packed her bag, and locked the door. She packed her bag and locked the door. (No subject after and, so no comma.)

Restrictive vs. nonrestrictive — and that vs. which

If the clause is essential to identify what you are talking about, use that and no commas. If it is extra information you could delete without confusion, use which and bracket it with commas.

The novel that won the prize is sold out. (Which novel? The clause is doing the identifying — essential, no commas.)

Her latest novel, which won the prize, is sold out. (You already know which novel; the prize is a bonus fact — nonessential, commas.) A quick test: if you can lift the clause out and the sentence still points at the same thing, it needs commas.

Appositives

An appositive renames the noun beside it. If it is extra information, surround it with commas. My neighbor, a retired pilot, tells the best stories. If it is essential to identify the noun, use no commas: The poet Maya Angelou wrote it. (Without "Maya Angelou" you would not know which poet.) The trap is a comma on one side and nothing on the other — an appositive needs commas on both ends or neither.

The semicolon

A semicolon needs a complete sentence on both sides. It works exactly like a period. The experiment failed; the data were still useful. If either side is not a full sentence, the semicolon is wrong. The one exception: use semicolons to separate list items that already contain commas. The panel included Dana, the captain; Luis, the goalie; and Priya, the striker.

The colon

A colon needs a complete sentence before it. After it can come a list, a single word, or an explanation. She had one goal: to finish. She wanted to: finish, rest, and celebrate. (No complete sentence before the colon.) She wanted three things: to finish, to rest, and to celebrate.

Dashes

A pair of dashes brackets an interruption, just like a pair of commas but with more emphasis. The results—surprising as they were—held up under review. A single dash can attach an explanation or afterthought to a complete sentence: She finally found it—her grandmother’s ring.

You cannot mix a dash with a comma around the same interrupter. If it opens with a dash, it closes with a dash; if it opens with a comma, it closes with a comma. The results—surprising as they were, held up. The results—surprising as they were—held up.

Apostrophes

its vs. it’s: it’s only ever means "it is" or "it has." Possessive its has no apostrophe. The dog wagged it’s tail. The dog wagged its tail.

Plural vs. possessive: a plural noun takes no apostrophe. The Smith’s are on vacation. The Smiths are on vacation. To show the family owns something, make it plural first, then add the apostrophe: the Smiths’ house.

Singular vs. plural possessive: the student’s locker (one student) vs. the students’ lockers (many students).

The five-way punctuation question

The ACT's favorite move is to give you the same spot with a comma, a semicolon, a colon, a dash, and nothing at all, and ask which is correct. Run this procedure in order and you will never guess:

The decision procedure

Step 1. Is there a complete sentence on both sides? If yes, a period, semicolon, or dash all work grammatically — and since only one can be the answer, look at meaning (does the second part explain the first? then a colon or dash) or just take the choice offered.
Step 2. Is there a complete sentence before but not after? A colon or a single dash can attach the fragment.
Step 3. Is one side not a complete sentence? Then a semicolon is out, and a period is out. You are choosing between a comma and no punctuation.
Step 4. If nothing needs separating — subject and verb, verb and object, a two-item pair — the answer is no punctuation.

Usage and agreement

Subject–verb agreement across an interrupting phrase

The ACT drops a prepositional phrase between the subject and the verb so your ear grabs the wrong noun. Mentally delete the phrase and the error is obvious. The box of chocolates were expensive. The box of chocolates was expensive. (The subject is box, not chocolates.)

Agreement in inverted sentences

When the sentence starts with there or here, the real subject comes after the verb — find it before you choose. There is several reasons to start now. There are several reasons to start now. (Subject: reasons.)

Pronoun–antecedent agreement

A pronoun must match the noun it replaces in number. The ACT treats each, every, everyone, and neither as singular. Each student must bring their own calculator. Each student must bring his or her own calculator. If "his or her" is not offered, the fix is usually to make the antecedent plural: Students must bring their own calculators.

Pronoun case: who / whom and the he/him trick

Answer the buried question with he or him. If he fits, use who; if him fits, use whom. The tutor whom helped me was patient. (He helped me → who.) The tutor who helped me was patient. To whom did you give the notes? (You gave them to him → whom.)

Verb tense sequence and consistency

Keep tenses consistent unless the meaning demands a shift, and use the past perfect (had + verb) for the earlier of two past events. By the time we arrived, the show already started. By the time we arrived, the show had already started.

Idiomatic prepositions

Certain words demand certain prepositions; there is no rule, only usage. She is capable to solve it on her own. She is capable of solving it on her own. Other common pairs the ACT tests: interested in, different from, preferable to, consists of, responsible for.

Comparative vs. superlative

Use the comparative (-er / more) for two things and the superlative (-est / most) for three or more. Of the two routes, this one is the fastest. Of the two routes, this one is the faster.

Adjective vs. adverb

An adverb modifies the verb and usually ends in -ly. She sings real good. She sings really well. (Really modifies well; well modifies sings.)

Commonly confused words

affect / effect: affect is usually the verb, effect the noun. The delay affected morale; the effect lasted weeks.
fewer / less: fewer for things you can count, less for amounts. There were less people than expected. There were fewer people than expected.
than / then: than compares; then is time. She is taller than I am; back then she was shorter.
between / among: between for two, among for three or more.
amount / number: number for countable things, amount for uncountable. a number of errors, an amount of effort.

Sentence structure

Structure questions are worth extra attention for you, because at a 28 your ear catches obvious fragments but misses the two disguised errors below: the participial fragment and the dangling modifier. Master those two and you pick up points most 28s leave behind.

Fragments — including the one that looks like a sentence

A fragment is missing a subject, a real verb, or a complete thought. The sneaky version uses an -ing word where a real verb belongs. Running through the rain without an umbrella. (No subject, no main verb — a participial phrase pretending to be a sentence.) She ran through the rain without an umbrella.

Run-ons and comma splices

A run-on jams two sentences together with no punctuation; a comma splice glues them with only a comma. Both are fixed the same four ways as any comma splice (period, semicolon, comma + FANBOYS, or subordination). The lecture ran long we missed the bus. The lecture ran long, so we missed the bus.

Misplaced modifiers

A modifier should sit next to the word it describes. Moved, it changes the meaning. She almost drove her kids to school every day. (She nearly drove — but didn't?) She drove her kids to school almost every day.

Dangling modifiers — the classic test

When a sentence opens with a descriptive phrase and a comma, the noun right after the comma must be the thing doing that action. If it is not, the modifier dangles. Walking to school, the rain soaked my jacket. (The rain was not walking to school.) Walking to school, I got my jacket soaked by the rain. or As I walked to school, the rain soaked my jacket.

On the test, when a choice rearranges the words after an opening phrase, check the first noun. This one rule answers a whole category of questions.

Faulty parallelism

Items in a list or on either side of a paired conjunction must share the same grammatical form. She likes hiking, swimming, and to bike. She likes hiking, swimming, and biking.

Paired constructions — not only…but also, either…or, both…and — must be parallel across the pair. She is not only a talented writer but also she edits well. She is not only a talented writer but also a skilled editor.

Shifts in construction

Do not switch person, number, or voice midstream. When one studies daily, you retain more. When you study daily, you retain more.

Production of Writing — what separates 28 from 32

This is the section to reread the night before the test. These questions look like reading questions, so students answer them by taste. But they have real rules, and once you see the rules they become the most reliable points on the page. Slow down here.

"Which choice is most specific / most vivid?"

The answer is the option with concrete detail that matches the stated criterion — not the prettiest or most sophisticated sentence. If the question asks for the most specific description of a workshop, the event was a really memorable experience loses to the three-hour welding demonstration drew forty students. Pick nouns and numbers, not adjectives and mood.

"The writer wants to [purpose]. Which choice best accomplishes that?"

Answer the stated purpose literally. If the writer wants to emphasize the speed of a process, the correct choice mentions speed — even if another option is more elegant. Underline the goal word in the question and find the choice that delivers exactly that word’s idea. Ignore whether the sentence is nice.

Add / delete questions

"Should the writer make this addition?" or "Should the underlined sentence be kept or deleted?" The yes/no turns entirely on relevance to the paragraph’s topic: keep it if it develops the paragraph’s point, cut it if it wanders, repeats, or introduces something off-topic. And here is the part students miss — the reason attached to each answer choice matters as much as the yes/no. A choice can say "Yes" for a true reason or "Yes" for a false one. Read all the way to the end of every option.

Transitions

A transition question is a logic question. Before you look at the choices, read the sentence before and the sentence after, and decide out loud what the relationship is: Are they agreeing? Contrasting? Is the second one a result of the first? Only then pick the word that names that relationship. Choosing a transition by ear is how strong students lose easy points.

Sentence and paragraph ordering

When a question asks where a sentence belongs, or the best order for sentences, use pronouns and transition words as anchors. A sentence that starts with "This decision" must come after the decision is named. A sentence beginning "However" must follow the idea it contradicts. A pronoun like they must have a plural noun already introduced before it. Track those threads and the order assembles itself.

The whole-essay goal question

The last question of a passage often asks whether the essay accomplishes some stated goal ("Does this essay fulfill the writer’s goal of describing a scientific breakthrough?"). Compare the essay’s actual scope against the goal. If the essay described a person’s career, it did not "describe a breakthrough," no matter how good it was. And — same rule as add/delete — both halves of the answer must be true: the Yes/No and the reason. A correct yes/no with a wrong justification is a wrong answer.

Redundancy and concision — the single most repeated trick

The ACT plants a phrase that says the same thing twice and hopes it sounds natural. The correct answer cuts the repetition, and it is frequently the choice that just deletes the underlined words. Train your eye on these:

The reason she left was because she felt sick. She left because she felt sick.
Each and every applicant was interviewed. Every applicant was interviewed.
They met at 6 a.m. in the morning. They met at 6 a.m.
The two ideas were combined together. The two ideas were combined.
It was a completely new innovation. It was an innovation.

When two choices are both grammatical and one is shorter with no lost meaning, take the shorter one.

Worked example: a "most specific" question end to end

The community garden started small. In its first spring, 1a handful of volunteers did some work on the empty lot.

Question 1. The writer wants to give the reader a specific picture of the garden’s beginnings. Which choice best accomplishes this?

A. NO CHANGE
B. some people got the project going in a meaningful way.
C. six neighbors cleared broken glass and tilled forty feet of soil.
D. things slowly began to happen on the property.

Answer: C. The stated criterion is a specific picture. C gives you a number (six), an action (cleared glass, tilled soil), and a measurement (forty feet). A, B, and D are all vague — "did some work," "in a meaningful way," "things began to happen." B sounds thoughtful and D sounds atmospheric, but neither shows you anything. On these questions, concrete detail beats nice phrasing every time.

The specific traps

These are the wrong answers the ACT reaches for again and again. Learn to recognize the pattern and you stop falling for it.

1. Fixes the error, adds a new one

A choice corrects the tested comma but changes a verb tense in the process. If a choice repairs the obvious problem, reread the whole option for a fresh mistake. Having finished the exam, relief washed over her. corrects an awkward phrase but dangles the modifier.

2. "Being" is almost always wrong

Being creates fragments and wordiness on the ACT more than it fixes anything. The reason for the delay being a storm. The delay was caused by a storm. When you see being in an answer, be suspicious first.

3. The tempting-but-wordy choice

The longest option often reads as the most "complete" and feels safest. It is usually padded. at this point in time vs. now. When a choice is longer for no added meaning, cross it off.

4. Grammatical but changes the meaning

A choice can be perfectly correct English yet say something the passage did not mean. Swapping because for although flips a cause into a contrast. Correct grammar is necessary, not sufficient — the meaning has to fit too.

5. Comma before "and" with no second sentence

A comma before and is only right when a full independent clause follows. She revised the draft, and submitted it. She revised the draft and submitted it. No subject after and means no comma.

6. Right yes/no, wrong reason

On add/delete and essay-goal questions, the correct Yes or No is often paired with a false justification. Both halves must be true. If the reason misdescribes the passage, the answer is wrong even when the yes/no is right.

Your routine

You do not need to grind grammar drills for a month. You need targeted reps on the two or three question types you currently miss, plus timed passages so your pace stays honest. Here is how English fits your schedule.

Every study day

One timed English passage (7 minutes), then review every question you missed or guessed on — a lucky guess is a future miss. Log the rule you broke.

Twice a week

A focused set of rhetoric questions — transitions, add/delete, "which choice" — because that is where your three points live. Do the punctuation drills only until you can run the decision procedure without thinking.

The last week

Reread the Production of Writing and Traps sections. Take no new material after the Wednesday before the test.

The one thing to remember

Grammar questions you already handle on feel. The points you are missing are in the questions that look like reading — transitions, relevance, concision, "which choice." Slow down on those, answer the question that is actually being asked, and take the shortest correct option. That is your path from 28 to 31.
