Cables and connectors

Recognize the connector, know what it carries, and remember the speed or limit.

On the exam, cable questions usually give you a device, a connector shape, or a speed. Your job is to map it to the right physical standard without overthinking it.

Connector or standard Use Speed, limit, or exam cue
USB-A Host-side USB connector on desktops, laptops, chargers, and hubs. Shape does not define speed; color sometimes hints, but the controller standard decides.
USB-B Device-side connector on printers, scanners, and older external drives. Common printer cable answer.
USB-C Reversible connector for data, charging, video alternate modes, and docks. Can carry USB, DisplayPort Alt Mode, Thunderbolt, and power delivery when supported.
Micro-USB Older mobile devices, small peripherals, and embedded gear. Small trapezoid; not reversible.
Mini-USB Older cameras, GPS units, and legacy peripherals. Larger than Micro-USB; mostly legacy.
USB 2.0 Keyboard, mouse, printer, basic flash drives. 480 Mbps.
USB 3.2 Gen 1 External storage and modern peripherals. 5 Gbps; same speed as older USB 3.0 / USB 3.1 Gen 1 naming.
USB 3.2 Gen 2 Higher-speed external SSDs and docks. 10 Gbps.
USB 3.2 Gen 2×2 High-speed USB-C external storage. 20 Gbps; requires USB-C and compatible host/device.
Thunderbolt 3 High-end docks, displays, external GPUs, fast storage. 40 Gbps over USB-C connector.
Thunderbolt 4 Modern docks and displays with stricter feature requirements. 40 Gbps over USB-C; more consistent minimum capabilities than Thunderbolt 3.
Lightning Apple mobile devices and accessories. Proprietary reversible Apple connector.
DisplayPort PC monitors and docking stations. Carries digital video and audio; supports daisy-chaining with MST.
HDMI TVs, monitors, projectors, game consoles, conference rooms. Carries digital video and audio; common home theater answer.
DVI Older flat-panel monitors. Digital video on DVI-D, analog on DVI-A, both on DVI-I; no native audio in normal PC use.
VGA Legacy projectors and monitors. Analog video only; 15-pin blue connector is a classic cue.
SATA revision 1 Internal drives. 1.5 Gbps.
SATA revision 2 Internal drives. 3 Gbps.
SATA revision 3 Internal HDDs, SATA SSDs, optical drives. 6 Gbps; common modern SATA number.
eSATA External SATA storage. Data only; external connector, not USB.
Molex Legacy power for older drives, fans, and adapters. 4-pin peripheral power connector.
SCSI Legacy servers, scanners, and enterprise storage. Older parallel storage bus; expect termination and device IDs in legacy contexts.
RJ11 Telephone lines and DSL phone connection. Smaller than RJ45; usually 2 or 4 conductors.
RJ45 Ethernet twisted-pair networking. 8P8C modular plug; used with Cat 5e, Cat 6, and Cat 6a.
F-type Coaxial cable for cable internet, cable TV, and satellite. Threaded coax connector.
Punchdown Terminating twisted pair into patch panels and keystone jacks. Use a punchdown tool, not a crimper.

Fiber essentials

Single-mode fiber

Uses a narrow core and laser light for long-distance runs. It is the long-range, higher-cost answer.

Multimode fiber

Uses a larger core and LED or laser light for shorter building or campus runs. It is usually cheaper than single-mode.

SC, LC, and ST

SC is square push-pull, LC is smaller with a latch, and ST is round with a twist-lock bayonet.

Exam trap

Connector shape and speed standard are not the same thing. USB-C can be slow or fast. M.2 can be SATA or NVMe. RJ45 is the connector; Cat 5e, Cat 6, and Cat 6a are cable categories.

Another favorite: Cat 6 supports 10 Gbps only to 55 m, while Cat 6a supports 10 Gbps to 100 m.

RAM

Match the memory generation, form factor, pin count, and compatibility rule.

RAM questions are mostly recognition. If the pin count, voltage, and notch do not match the board, the module does not install. Do not try to force a memory generation into the wrong slot.

Memory Desktop DIMM pins Laptop SODIMM pins Voltage Common speed range Keying rule
DDR3 240 204 1.5 V standard; DDR3L is 1.35 V DDR3-800 to DDR3-2133 Different notch from DDR4 and DDR5.
DDR4 288 260 1.2 V DDR4-2133 to DDR4-3200 commonly Same desktop pin count as DDR5, but different notch and signaling.
DDR5 288 262 1.1 V DDR5-4800 and higher commonly Not physically interchangeable with DDR4 despite 288-pin desktop count.

DIMM vs SODIMM

DIMMs go in desktops and many servers. SODIMMs are shorter modules used in laptops, mini PCs, and some all-in-one systems.

Channels

Single channel uses one memory channel. Dual channel and quad channel increase bandwidth when the CPU and motherboard support it and modules are installed in the correct matched slots.

Matched pairs matter because the memory controller runs best when each channel has the same capacity and similar speed/timing. Mixing sizes can work, but part of the memory may run in single-channel mode or at the slowest module’s speed.

ECC memory detects and corrects single-bit memory errors. It belongs in servers, workstations, and systems where data integrity matters. Non-ECC memory is normal for consumer desktops and laptops.

Virtual memory uses storage as an overflow area when physical RAM is insufficient. On Windows, that backing file is the page file. It prevents immediate crashes but is much slower than RAM, especially on HDDs.

Most testable RAM facts

DDR3 desktop DIMM is 240 pins. DDR4 and DDR5 desktop DIMMs are both 288 pins, but they are keyed differently and are not interchangeable.

Laptop pin counts are DDR3 SODIMM 204, DDR4 SODIMM 260, and DDR5 SODIMM 262. ECC is a server/workstation feature, not a gaming-speed feature.

Quick installation checklist
  1. Check motherboard generation support first.
  2. Check supported capacity per slot and total capacity.
  3. Install matched modules in the paired slots shown in the motherboard manual.
  4. Enable the supported memory profile only if the board and RAM support it.
  5. If the system fails POST, reseat RAM and test one module at a time.

Storage

Separate form factor from bus and memorize RAID behavior.

Storage questions often hide the answer in the bus. A drive can physically be M.2 but electrically SATA or NVMe. NVMe uses PCIe lanes and is much faster than SATA because it was designed for solid-state storage, not spinning disks.

HDD

Spinning magnetic storage. 5400 RPM drives use less power and are common in laptops; 7200 RPM drives are faster and common in desktops. 2.5-inch fits laptops; 3.5-inch fits desktops.

SSD

No moving parts, low latency, and high random I/O. Common form factors include 2.5-inch SATA, mSATA, M.2, and U.2.

Optical

CD, DVD, and Blu-ray drives are used for legacy media, installation discs, and archival workflows. They are usually SATA internally or USB externally.

M.2 keying and bus

Form factor Bus Keying Exam point
2.5-inch SATA SSD SATA SATA data and power Limited by SATA III at 6 Gbps before overhead.
mSATA SATA Mini PCIe-like connector Older small SSD format.
M.2 SATA SATA Often B key or B+M key M.2 shape does not guarantee NVMe speed.
M.2 NVMe PCIe / NVMe Often M key Uses PCIe lanes; much higher throughput and lower latency than SATA.
U.2 PCIe / NVMe Enterprise cabled connector Common in servers and enterprise storage.

NVMe over PCIe can use multiple lanes, commonly PCIe x4 for client SSDs. A PCIe 3.0 x4 NVMe drive can move several GB/s, while SATA SSDs top out around the SATA interface limit. That real throughput advantage matters for large file transfers, boot times, and heavy I/O workloads.

RAID levels

RAID Minimum drives Usable capacity formula Fault tolerance When used
RAID 0 2 Number of drives × smallest drive None Speed and capacity when data loss is acceptable.
RAID 1 2 Smallest drive One drive in a mirror can fail Simple redundancy for boot volumes or small systems.
RAID 5 3 (Number of drives − 1) × smallest drive One drive can fail Balanced capacity and redundancy in small arrays.
RAID 6 4 (Number of drives − 2) × smallest drive Two drives can fail Larger arrays needing better rebuild safety.
RAID 10 4 Half of total raw capacity Can lose one drive per mirror pair High performance plus redundancy; striped mirrors.
Exam trap

RAID is not a backup. RAID keeps a system available after a drive failure, but it does not protect against deletion, malware, theft, fire, or corrupted files.

Power supplies

Know connector pin counts, sizing, safety, and rails.

Pick a PSU by estimating CPU, GPU, drives, fans, and peripherals, then add headroom. A common target is enough capacity that the system does not run at the PSU’s maximum output during normal load.

Connector or feature Use What to remember
24-pin ATX Main motherboard power. Largest motherboard power connector.
8-pin EPS / CPU CPU power near the processor socket. Do not confuse with PCIe GPU power.
6+2-pin PCIe Graphics card auxiliary power. Can function as 6-pin or 8-pin for GPUs.
SATA power SATA drives and some accessories. Flat 15-pin power connector.
Molex Legacy peripherals. 4-pin connector; still appears in adapter questions.
Modular PSU All cables detachable. Better cable management; use cables designed for that exact PSU model.
Semi-modular PSU Some fixed cables, some detachable. Main motherboard cables are often fixed.
Non-modular PSU All cables permanently attached. Cheaper, but more unused cables in the case.
Redundant PSU Server availability. One PSU can fail while the system stays powered.
80 PLUS Efficiency rating. Higher ratings waste less power as heat; not a wattage rating.

Voltage and rails

In North America, input voltage is commonly 115 V. Many other regions use 230 V. Older PSUs may have a red voltage selector switch; setting it wrong can damage equipment. Modern active PFC supplies usually auto-switch.

Rail Feeds Exam cue
+3.3 V Chipsets, memory-related circuitry, and low-voltage logic. Motherboard logic.
+5 V USB power, some drive electronics, and logic circuits. USB and legacy logic.
+12 V CPU voltage regulators, GPU, motors, fans, and drives. High-draw components.
Safety warning

Never open a power supply. Capacitors can retain dangerous charge after the system is unplugged. Replace the PSU as a unit unless you are trained and authorized to service it.

Motherboards, CPUs, and cooling

Read compatibility from form factor, socket, chipset, firmware, and thermal design.
Form factor Typical dimensions Expansion profile Use case
ATX 12 × 9.6 in Up to 7 expansion slots Full desktop builds with multiple cards.
microATX 9.6 × 9.6 in Up to 4 expansion slots Smaller desktops with moderate expansion.
mini-ITX 6.7 × 6.7 in Usually 1 expansion slot Small form factor systems.

PCIe slots are described by lane count: x1, x4, x8, and x16. A shorter PCIe card can fit in a longer slot, such as an x1 card in an x16 slot. A physically longer card cannot fit in a shorter closed slot. PCIe generations are backward compatible, but speed drops to the slowest supported generation and lane count.

Headers

Front-panel headers connect power switch, reset switch, power LED, and drive LED. USB headers feed front-panel USB ports. Fan headers power and control fans. M.2 sockets accept small SSDs or wireless cards depending on board support.

BIOS vs UEFI

BIOS is legacy firmware. UEFI adds Secure Boot, GPT support, boot drives larger than 2 TB, graphical setup, and better preboot features.

CMOS battery

A dead CMOS battery causes lost date/time, forgotten firmware settings, and sometimes boot-order resets after power is removed.

TPM stores cryptographic keys and supports measured boot, device encryption, and platform identity. It may be a discrete chip or firmware TPM built into the CPU/chipset platform.

CPU socket compatibility is physical and electrical. LGA puts pins in the socket and pads on the CPU. PGA puts pins on the CPU and holes in the socket. Socket shape, chipset support, BIOS/UEFI version, and power delivery all matter.

Cores are physical execution units. Threads are logical execution paths. Virtualization extensions such as Intel VT-x and AMD-V let hypervisors run virtual machines efficiently.

Cooling removes heat from the CPU, GPU, chipset, and case. Heat sinks spread heat, thermal paste fills microscopic gaps, and thermal pads bridge wider gaps on components such as VRAM. Air cooling uses fans and heat sinks; liquid cooling moves heat to a radiator. Fan curves adjust fan speed based on temperature.

Compatibility order

For a CPU upgrade, check socket first, then chipset support, firmware version, power requirements, cooler clearance, and memory support.

Printers

Memorize the laser imaging process in exact order and match symptoms to printer type.

Laser imaging process

  1. Processing: The printer receives and rasterizes the page; failures look like slow printing, garbled output, or job errors.
  2. Charging: The primary charge roller applies a uniform charge to the drum; failures cause gray background or uneven image density.
  3. Exposing: A laser writes the image by discharging selected drum areas; failures cause missing or distorted image areas.
  4. Developing: Toner sticks to the discharged image areas on the drum; failures cause faded print or missing toner.
  5. Transferring: Toner moves from drum to paper; failures cause blank pages, weak image transfer, or toner left on the drum.
  6. Fusing: Heat and pressure bond toner to the paper; failures cause toner that smears or rubs off.
  7. Cleaning: Residual toner is removed and the drum is prepared for the next page; failures cause repeating marks or ghost images.

Inkjet

Uses a print head, carriage, roller, duplexing assembly, and calibration. Common issues include clogged nozzles, streaks, alignment problems, and wet ink smearing.

Thermal

Uses a feed assembly, heating element, and special thermal paper. Receipt printers are the classic example; heat or old paper can darken output.

Impact

Uses a print head, ribbon, tractor feed, and impact paper. It is still used for multipart forms because physical impact transfers through carbon or multipart sheets.

3-D

Uses filament or resin, a print bed, motors, and calibration. Failed adhesion, clogged nozzles, and leveling problems are common symptoms.

Consumables and maintenance

Printer type Consumables Maintenance interval cue Common maintenance task
Laser Toner, drum, fuser, maintenance kit. Page-count based. Replace toner, drum, fuser, transfer roller, pickup rollers.
Inkjet Ink cartridges or tanks, print head. When quality drops or after cartridge changes. Clean nozzles, align/calibrate, replace cartridges.
Thermal Thermal paper; sometimes ribbon for thermal transfer. Roll replacement or poor output. Clean heating element and feed path.
Impact Ribbon and paper. When print is faint or forms misfeed. Replace ribbon, align tractor feed, clear jams.
3-D Filament, resin, build surface. Per material change or print failure. Level bed, clean nozzle, clean resin vat, calibrate axes.

Printer sharing lets a computer share its attached printer. A print server manages print queues for network printers. Spooling stores print jobs on disk so applications can finish sending the job before the printer is physically done.

Printer trap

If toner smears after printing, think fuser. If ink streaks or colors are missing on an inkjet, think clogged nozzles or calibration. If multipart forms are required, choose impact.

Displays and video

Connect display symptoms to panel technology, backlight, resolution, and port capability.
Panel type Strengths Weaknesses Best fit
IPS Good color and wide viewing angles. Usually slower response than TN and may cost more. Design, office work, general use.
TN Fast response and low cost. Weak viewing angles and color accuracy. Budget displays or competitive gaming.
VA Strong contrast and deep blacks. Can have slower pixel transitions. Media viewing and general use.
OLED Excellent contrast, true blacks, fast response. Burn-in risk and higher cost. Premium laptops, phones, and displays.

Resolution is the pixel count, such as 1920 × 1080 or 3840 × 2160. Aspect ratio is the shape, such as 16:9 or 16:10. Refresh rate is how often the display updates per second, measured in Hz. Response time is how quickly pixels change state.

LCD panels need a backlight. LED backlights are common now. Older CCFL-backlit laptops used an inverter to convert power for the fluorescent tube; a failed inverter could cause a very dim image that is barely visible with a flashlight.

The night-before hardware sheet

Pure recall to review right before the exam.

RAID minimums

  • RAID 0: 2 drives, no fault tolerance.
  • RAID 1: 2 drives, mirror.
  • RAID 5: 3 drives, one-drive fault tolerance.
  • RAID 6: 4 drives, two-drive fault tolerance.
  • RAID 10: 4 drives, striped mirrors.

DDR pin counts

  • DDR3 DIMM: 240; DDR3 SODIMM: 204.
  • DDR4 DIMM: 288; DDR4 SODIMM: 260.
  • DDR5 DIMM: 288; DDR5 SODIMM: 262.
  • DDR generations are keyed differently.

USB and Thunderbolt

  • USB 2.0: 480 Mbps.
  • USB 3.2 Gen 1: 5 Gbps.
  • USB 3.2 Gen 2: 10 Gbps.
  • USB 3.2 Gen 2×2: 20 Gbps.
  • Thunderbolt 3/4: 40 Gbps.

Laser order

  1. Processing
  2. Charging
  3. Exposing
  4. Developing
  5. Transferring
  6. Fusing
  7. Cleaning

PSU connectors

  • 24-pin ATX: motherboard.
  • 8-pin EPS: CPU.
  • 6+2-pin PCIe: GPU.
  • SATA power: drives.
  • Molex: legacy peripherals.

Cat cable limits

  • Cat 5: 100 Mbps / 100 m.
  • Cat 5e: 1 Gbps / 100 m.
  • Cat 6: 10 Gbps / 55 m or 1 Gbps / 100 m.
  • Cat 6a: 10 Gbps / 100 m.
